# Short-form horror: hooks, retention and series craft

A story and structure layer. It decides what the viewer sees and hears second by second, and how a part of a series
works for someone who has never seen the series. Production (narration, animation, rendering, QA) stays with
`remotion-video-studio`; the look stays with `gothic-pov-style`. When they disagree on facts, rights or honest
verification, those skills win.

**Evidence level.** The platform advice below comes mostly from practitioner and marketing sources, and their numbers
conflict. Treat thresholds as starting points to test against the account's own Insights, never as platform rules.
The craft lessons (sections 7–9) come from making THE WATCH (Parts I–III) and *The Quarter Turn* in this repo.

## 1. The first three seconds decide everything

Most viewers who leave do so in the first seconds. Instagram reports this early drop-off directly (a 3-second skip
rate). Write the opening first and spend the most effort on it.

Do:
- **Open on the most charged image you have,** not on setup. A hand, a door, a mark, a reflection.
- **Speak the hook in the same breath as the image.** Picture and voice land together within 1.5–3 s.
- **Make the hook carry the whole premise.** A stranger must understand who, what's wrong, and why it matters
  from the first line alone: *"My best mate's a werewolf. So am I. And he's just let himself in."*
- **Test the first 1.5 s on mute.** If the frame means nothing without sound, change the frame, not the caption.
- **Use one of the proven shapes:** a contrarian statement, a confession, an impossible fact, a rule with a threat
  (*"Never fix the kitchen tap"*), or an in-progress crisis.

Don't:
- greet, introduce yourself, or ease in with mood shots;
- open on a title card, a black screen, a logo or a recap slide;
- put a fact the hook depends on in the caption only (captions often arrive late or get skipped).

## 2. Write for the viewer who missed every earlier part

Every part is someone's first part. TV solves this with a 15–30 s "previously on"; a reel has no room for that.

1. **Make each part complete:** its own question, its own answer, its own new cliffhanger. A part that only works
   after watching the others loses every newcomer.
2. **Recap in one line, inside the story, after the hook.** Phrase it so returning viewers hear a reminder and new
   viewers hear context: *"Last week, I found out he took his uncle's arm. And told me I did it."* Show the proof
   object from the earlier part (the notebook page, the text message) under that line.
3. **Never start with the recap.** Hook → one-line recap → new story.
4. **Label the part quietly:** a small "SERIES · Part N" marker for the first ~4 s (upper corner, outside the hook's
   focal point), and the full title card at the end. Number parts in the post caption too.
5. **Make the first line work as a standalone sentence.** Avoid "So…", "Then…", "After that…" openings that
   presuppose the last part.

## 3. A ladder of open loops

Curiosity comes from noticing a gap between what you know and what you want to know (Loewenstein's
information-gap theory). Keep one gap open at all times and refresh it often.

- **Open a small question every 5–10 s, and close the previous one as you open the next.** Write the ladder out
  before the script:
  `Why is he here? → What's the nail for? → Which house? → Why the holes? → Will he go?`
- **Make every payoff match its setup.** An unpaid or inflated loop reads as clickbait and costs the next part.
- **Use a roll-call or countdown for the middle** (house two, house four, even Danny's…). Repetition builds a rhythm
  that makes the break in the pattern (*"Except one."*) hit harder.
- **Subvert one expectation the earlier parts created** (*"Even Danny's house. Silver."*). Returning viewers feel
  rewarded; new viewers just feel the surprise.

## 4. Structure that works (30–65 s)

| Beat | ~60 s timing | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hook | 0–3 s | Charged image + premise line |
| Recap (series only) | 3–9 s | One line over a proof object from the earlier part |
| Rule / situation | 9–25 s | The folklore rule or the threat; plant one spoken clue and one visual clue |
| Escalation | 25–40 s | The test, the search, the roll-call; loops open and close fast |
| Turn | 40–50 s | The rule turns on the narrator, readable with the sound off |
| Proof | 50–55 s | One undeniable image and ONE line that reinterprets what came before |
| Cliffhanger | 55–60 s | An unfinished action, hard cut to black, a whispered last line, title |

Rules for the twist:
- **Invert who the rule protected, or from whom** (the watcher was a protector; the friend is the danger).
- **Plant both clues before the turn.** One is spoken and sounds innocent; the other is visual only. On rewatch
  they should be obvious.
- **Explain the twist in one line, never more.** Never show the outcome of the cliffhanger.
- **Use real folklore viewers can name and argue about in the comments**: silver, running water, invitations,
  wolfsbane, counting, mirrors.

## 5. Endings: cliffhanger, echo, send

- **End on an unfinished action** (a hand reaching, a door opening, a voice behind you), then a hard cut to black.
- **Keep the whisper to eight words or fewer,** and have it echo an earlier part or the opening: *"Come out."*
  answers Part I's *"watching for one getting out."* An echo invites a rewatch; a loop back into the first frame does
  the same.
- **Give people something worth sending.** Sends (DM shares) are reported as the strongest driver of reach to
  non-followers, ahead of likes. A testable rule, a twist you want to spring on a friend, or a line people will quote
  all qualify.
- **Plant the next part inside this one,** not after it: a single line or image the next part pays off (*"His mum was at
  the window. Watching me count."*). It reads as atmosphere now and as a promise later.
- **Don't end with "follow for part 4".** A strong unanswered question does that job better.

## 6. Script rules for the narrator

- Use mostly 3–10 word sentences, one image each, with contractions and plain wording. Spell numbers out
  (*"nineteen seventy-four"*).
- Never write sound-effect words into the narration.
- Give reveal lines their own sentence and a long pause after them: 1.0–1.2 s after a reveal, 0.6–0.9 s before a
  scene change, 0.2–0.35 s between ordinary lines.
- Report other characters' dialogue through the narrator (*"He said, it's a full moon in an hour"*) unless a
  distinct voice is part of the twist (a letter, the last whisper).
- **Runtime:** aim for 60–65 s total for a series part. If the narration is long, cut words before speeding up the
  voice. If it's short, add a beat that plants or pays off something, never dead air.

## 7. Picture rules: what actually kills retention

These came from reviewing our own renders frame by frame. Each one is a mistake we made.

- **Check every new drawing at phone size.** Misreads we caught: snow-capped roses read as strawberries, claw drags
  as a comb then as flames, a pinned sleeve as a dial, three wolfsbane bundles as grapes, a front-on face as a bald
  ball. When something reads as something else, redraw it.
- **Silhouettes need light behind them.** A figure behind dark glass is invisible. Give the window a backlit room
  (warm lamp, cold torch) or the reveal doesn't exist.
- **Keep the subject inside the frame and off the caption band** (portrait: y 1330–1560 and the bottom 360 px).
  Check close-ups especially: our nail-holes close-up was framed below the sill, and a house plate sat behind its
  caption.
- **Light the darkest scenes enough to read.** Horror is dark, but a black frame is a skipped frame. Torch scenes
  need ambient light of about 0.12 or more, and the key object must sit inside the beam.
- **Check continuity between beats.** A door shown ajar before the story opens it breaks the logic for attentive
  viewers. Re-read the script against the shot list.
- **Show the key action early in its shot.** A hand that only enters the frame in the last 0.2 s before the cut
  isn't seen. Start movement soon after the cut-in.
- **Make the twist readable with the sound off:** handwriting on a page, a phone text, holes in a sill, a
  reflection, nails falling.

## 8. Captions

- Burn captions in. Many viewers watch muted, and the twist must survive it.
- Show one phrase at a time, at most two lines, split at measured pauses in the audio. Keep phrases to about 65
  characters or fewer.
- Make **one red word per beat at most**, and only on reveals (*werewolf. · one. · out.*). Check that the red word
  doesn't also appear in an ordinary line elsewhere in the script.
- Keep captions clear of faces, hands and key props. Captions over bright paper rely on their shadow; check them.

## 9. Voice

- **Pick a voice with stable pronunciation and pacing,** and keep it consistent within a series. In this project, the
  Kokoro `bm_lewis` voice was dependable. A cloned Chatterbox voice gave more character but produced takes the
  owner judged to have too many issues, so Part III went back to Kokoro. Whatever the engine, have a human listen to
  the full take before rendering. If you can't listen, say so.
- **Audition with the real lines,** including the whisper and the hardest names, before narrating everything.
- **Keep the old takes** when switching engines, and re-derive every cut from the new timing rather than
  hand-moving them.
- **Cast extra voices only where they carry the story:** a letter in the writer's voice, a final line from someone
  who shouldn't be there.

## 10. Before you render: checklist

- [ ] Hook image and premise line land within 3 s; the first 1.5 s reads on mute
- [ ] A newcomer understands the part with no earlier part (recap in one line, after the hook)
- [ ] The loop ladder is written out; a new question every 5–10 s; every payoff matches its setup
- [ ] One expectation from earlier parts is subverted (series parts)
- [ ] Spoken and visual clues are planted before the turn; the twist is explained in one line and readable on mute
- [ ] An unfinished action, a hard cut, a whisper of eight words or fewer that echoes something, a title
- [ ] One plant for the next part; one send-worthy moment
- [ ] Runtime 60–65 s; reveal pauses 1.0–1.2 s
- [ ] Every drawing checked at phone size; silhouettes backlit; no key action in the caption band; dark scenes readable
- [ ] One red word per beat, unique in the script; captions split at measured pauses
- [ ] Narration listened to by a person (or the limitation recorded in `qa.md`)
- [ ] A quiet part label for the first 4 s; full title card at the end

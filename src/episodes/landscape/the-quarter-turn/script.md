## COLD OPEN

Before she died, my grandmother made me promise one thing.

Never fix the kitchen tap.

I lasted nine days.

*[drip in silence · title card: THE QUARTER TURN]*

---

## CHAPTER I · THE PROMISE

Gran's house stands at the very end of Moor End, in Thornhallow, where the road gives up and the moor begins.

Stone walls. A slate roof that leaks in one corner. A garden that's mostly nettles, apart from the roses.

She grew them in a ring at the bottom of the garden. Red ones. They flowered in December. Nobody ever asked her how.

I spent every summer in that house as a boy. Mum worked, and Gran didn't mind having me.

She was strict about very little. Shoes off at the door. Grace before tea.

And the tap.

Every night, at eleven o'clock, she'd go into the kitchen in her dressing gown, take hold of the cold tap, and give it a quarter turn.

Not a half. Not a full turn. A quarter.

Then she'd stand there, very still, until it started to drip. One drop, every two seconds.

Only then would she go up to bed.

I asked her once why she didn't just get it fixed.

She said, some things want fixing. Some things want watching.

I was eight. I didn't understand.

There was one room I was never allowed in. The back bedroom, at the top of the stairs. It was always locked.

She said it was full of Grandad's things, and she couldn't face them.

Grandad walked out on her in the winter of nineteen seventy-four. He took nothing. He left no note. Nobody ever saw him again.

She never remarried. She never even took off her ring.

Everyone said I had her eyes. Grey, like the moor in the rain.

Gran died in October. She was eighty-six.

The last time I saw her, in the hospital, she took my wrist and held it so hard it bruised.

Promise me you won't fix that tap.

I promised. I'd have promised her anything.

She left me the house.

---

## CHAPTER II · NINE DAYS

I moved in the week after the funeral.

The house still smelt of her. Lavender, coal tar soap, and something underneath it, like a cellar after rain.

On the first night, I'd forgotten all about the tap, until I was lying in bed.

*[drip · two seconds · drip]*

It's a small sound, in daylight. At night, in an empty house, it fills every room.

I'd lie there counting. I couldn't stop. Seven hundred drops. Eight hundred.

By the third night, I was sleeping with a pillow over my head.

By the fifth, I'd started to notice something.

The drip wasn't steady.

On dark nights, when there was no moon over the garden, it came faster. As if the tap was nervous.

On bright nights, it slowed right down.

I told myself it was the pressure. Old pipes. Cold weather.

On the seventh day, I met the man next door. Mr Haworth. Ninety, if he was a day.

He watched me carrying bin bags down the path, and said, you'll be keeping the water going, then.

I said, sorry?

He said, your gran. She kept the water moving. Every night. Fifty-odd years.

I asked him what for.

He looked past me, down the garden, at the roses. Then he went inside and shut his door.

That night, I tried the back bedroom. Still locked. I searched the whole house for the key. Every drawer. Every tin. Every coat pocket.

Nothing.

By the ninth day, I hadn't slept properly in over a week. I was counting drops in my dreams.

And I thought, she's gone. It's a tap. It's a washer that costs forty pence.

So I rang a plumber.

---

## CHAPTER III · THE PLUMBER

He was young, and cheerful, and called Kieran. He had a van with a cartoon spanner on the side.

He took one look at the tap and laughed. Your gran's been doing this on purpose, he said. Washer's fine. It's just been opened a quarter turn.

Then he crouched under the sink with his torch, and went quiet.

That's odd, he said.

The cold pipe didn't go where it should. It didn't go down to the mains.

It went sideways. Through the back wall. Out into the garden.

We followed it with a spade. It ran a foot under the lawn, in a long, careful curve.

All the way round the rose bed.

And back into the house, to the drain under the sink.

A complete circle.

Kieran stood up and scratched his head. Somebody's built a ring of running water round your roses, he said. Like a moat. Never seen anything like it.

He said it was old. Copper. Seventies, by the look of the joints.

Nineteen seventy-four, I thought. And I didn't know why.

He asked me what I wanted done.

I should have said, leave it.

I said, can you just make it stop.

He capped the pipe at the wall. Ten minutes. Thirty quid.

And the house went quiet.

For the first time in fifty-two years, nothing in that kitchen moved.

I stood there for a long time, listening to the silence.

I remember thinking how peaceful it was.

---

## CHAPTER IV · THE QUIET

That night, I slept like the dead. Deep, and black, and dreamless.

I woke at three. I didn't know why.

Then I did.

There was no drip. There would never be a drip again. And somewhere in the house, something else had taken its place.

A sound, downstairs. Slow. Heavy. Like someone stamping the mud off their boots.

I went down with my phone torch.

The back door was shut. Locked. The key on its hook.

But there was soil on the kitchen floor.

Wet, dark soil. And rose petals. Red ones.

A trail of it, from the back door, across the kitchen, to the bottom of the stairs.

And up them.

I followed it, one stair at a time. Every step had a print on it. A man's boot. An old-fashioned heel.

It stopped at the top of the stairs.

At the back bedroom.

The door was open.

Fifty-two years locked, and now it stood open, just a crack, and cold air was coming out of it.

I pushed it.

Inside, it was nineteen seventy-four.

A brown armchair. A calendar on the wall, turned to December. A man's coat laid out on the bed, as if he'd only just taken it off.

A wedding photo on the bedside table. Gran, young, laughing. And next to her, a man in a dark suit, with my nose, and my chin.

The bed was dented. As if someone had been sitting on it.

And on the pillow, there was a letter. In Gran's handwriting.

On the envelope, one word.

My name.

## CHAPTER V · THE QUARTER TURN

*[the letter: same narrator, or Gran's own voice: see question 1]*

If you're reading this, you fixed the tap. Don't be cross with yourself. I always knew you would. You never could leave a thing alone.

I'm sorry I lied to you about your grandad. He didn't walk out.

In the winter of seventy-four, he came home late across the moor. Something had followed him. By the next night, he wasn't your grandad any more.

I did what had to be done. I put him in the garden. I planted roses over him. And I laid the pipe myself.

Running water, love. It's the one thing they cannot cross. As long as the water moved, he couldn't rise.

So every night, I gave it a quarter turn.

Fifty-two years of quarter turns.

I'm not sorry. But I'm so tired.

If the water's stopped, he'll come home. He'll go to his room. He'll think it's still nineteen seventy-four.

And then, he'll look for me.

Don't let him see your face.

You look just like me.

*[end of letter]*

I read it twice. Then I ran.

Down the stairs. Into the kitchen. To the tap.

I took hold of it, the way she always did, and I gave it a quarter turn.

Nothing.

Another.

Nothing. Not a drop. Kieran had capped it at the wall.

And behind me, at the top of the stairs, a door opened.

Footsteps. Slow. Heavy. Coming down.

I stood in the dark, with my hand on a dry tap, and I didn't turn around.

The footsteps stopped, right behind me.

And a voice I'd never heard, in a house I'd known all my life, said,

*[whisper, a different voice]* Margaret? I'm home.
